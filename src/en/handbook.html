Before you start

How to use this handbook

This is the sustainable edition of the “bó jī” (lean physique) theory. Understand the goal, pick a program, and follow it. Every number here is a starting point, not a rule. Adjust to your own situation.

Check first

  • Heart disease, high blood pressure, diabetes, an old joint injury, pregnancy, or long-term medication: talk to a doctor first, especially before intervals and jumping.
  • Not sure? Fill in the free PAR-Q+ pre-exercise questionnaire.
  • Haven't trained in a long time? Keep everything light and short for the first 2–4 weeks.

The pain traffic light

Learn to tell “tired” from “hurt.” Sore muscles are normal. Sore joints are not.

Green: keep going
Muscle burn, fatigue, heavy breathing; muscle soreness 1–2 days after training.
Yellow: adjust
Mild joint discomfort (3 out of 10 or less) that is gone by the next day. Lower the weight, shorten the range, or swap to a pain-free exercise.
Red: stop
Sharp pain, pain that builds as you train or is worse the next day, numbness or swelling. Stop training that area and see a doctor or physical therapist.
The principles behind this handbook
  • Doing it for years beats doing it perfectly for a few weeks.
  • Watch trends, not single days.
  • On busy weeks, doing less still counts. Don't drop the whole week because you can't do it all.
Chapter 1

The goal: what “lean physique” means

Lean physique = enough muscle + moderate body fat. The aim is a body that looks good, works well, and is easy to keep, not a particular number.

There are only two levers

Strength training builds muscle. Managing calories brings body fat down. There is no special “lean physique” workout. Strength, cardio, and balance make that muscle quick, stable, and hard to injure.

Body fat guide

  • Everyday: about 10–15% for men and 20–25% for women. Most people can eat enough, train hard, and sleep well here.
  • Short-term dips: you can lean out briefly for a photo shoot or event, then return to everyday levels. This step is optional.
  • Body fat devices are imprecise: scales and handheld analyzers are often off by several percentage points. Waist size, photos, and long-term trends are more reliable.
A simpler health check

Keep your waist under half your height. At 170 cm (5′7″), aim for a waist under 85 cm (33.5″). Measure at the navel after a relaxed breath out.

Signals that matter more than body fat

These usually mean you're eating too little or training too much. They apply to men and women:

  • Irregular or missing periods, clearly lower sex drive
  • Always tired, often sick, injured again and again
  • Training performance keeps sliding

Eat a bit more and train a bit less. If it doesn't improve, see a doctor.

Chapter 2

Train six things, but not equally

Strength, cardio, and recovery are the three pillars. Give them real time. Power, balance, and mobility need only small doses, slotted into warm-ups and gaps in your program.

StrengthLooks, metabolism, staying independent as you ageCompound lifts, adding load slowly3 sessions
CardioHeart health, faster recovery, a longer lifeMostly moderate, a little vigorous150 min moderate or 75 min vigorous
RecoveryMakes the other five actually workSleep, deload weeks, managing stress7–9 hours a night

You only need enough flexibility to do each exercise properly. Extra stretching doesn't clearly prevent injuries, so don't spend a lot of time on it.

Chapter 3

Strength training: five rules

Learn these five and they work with any program.

#RuleNotes
1Train each muscle twice a weekWorks better than once a week, and missing a day doesn't wipe out the week.
2About 10 sets per muscle per weekBeginners grow on 6–8. Warm-up sets don't count.
3Stop each set with 1–3 reps left in the tankToo easy doesn't count; going to failure every set is too draining. See Figure 3.
4Main lifts 6–10 reps, accessories 8–15Main lifts are big moves like squats, deadlifts, bench press, and rows. Accessories are small moves like lateral raises and curls.
5Progress with “double progression”Add reps first, then weight. See Figure 4.

Double progression

Give each exercise a rep range, for example 8–10.

  1. Keep the weight the same and try to add a rep or two each session.
  2. Once every set reaches the top of the range (10), add weight next time and drop back to the bottom (8).
  3. How much to add: 1–2.5 kg (2.5–5 lb) for upper body, 2.5–5 kg (5–10 lb) for lower body. For small moves like lateral raises and curls, adding weight is hard; just add reps.

How each session runs

  1. Dynamic warm-up, 5–10 min (Chapter 6).
  2. Power work, 5–10 min (on the days it's scheduled).
  3. Main lifts: 2–3 warm-up sets building up in weight, then your work sets. Rest 2–3 min between sets.
  4. Accessories: rest 1–2 min; pair two exercises back to back (a superset) to save time.
  5. Stretch for 5 min and log today's weights and reps.

When you stall

Deload weeks

About every 6–8 weeks, or earlier if you're clearly worn out, your joints ache, or you keep stalling: halve your sets, keep the weight the same or a little lower, for one week. Then go back to normal.

Chapter 4

Pick a program

Three sessions a week is enough for most people. What matters is hitting each muscle twice, about 10 sets a week, and sticking with it for years.

Chapter 5

Cardio

About 150 minutes of moderate cardio a week, or 75 of vigorous, or a mix. Count by intensity, not by the name of the activity.

Use the talk test to judge intensity

A simple mix

  • Moderate: 2–3 times a week, 30–60 minutes each.
  • Vigorous: once a week. For example, 4 rounds of “4 min hard, 3 min easy,” about 30 minutes in all.
  • Daily steps: keep walking 7,000–10,000 steps a day. Walking fast enough that you can't sing counts toward your cardio.
  • Low fitness to start? Begin with incline walking. When it gets too easy to raise your heart rate, switch to jogging.
TypeProsWatch out
Incline walkingLowest impact, easiest way to reach moderateFit people may struggle to raise their heart rate
CyclingLow impact, least effect on leg trainingLittle stimulus for bones
JoggingEfficient, good for bone densityEach landing is 2–3× body weight; better not the day after legs
SwimmingAlmost zero impactLeast stimulus for bones
Sports, hikingFun, easy to stick withIntensity jumps around; a rough estimate is fine
Will cardio hurt muscle growth?

Not at sensible amounts. If muscle is the priority, lift first and do cardio after on the same day; a few hours apart is even better, but not required. Running interferes with leg training more than cycling does.

Chapter 6

Power, mobility, and balance

None of these needs its own session. Spend 5–10 minutes on them inside your warm-up or lifting.

Power: twice a week, 5–10 minutes, right after the warm-up

AreaExerciseSets
Lower bodyBox jump, standing broad jump3 × 3–5
Upper bodyMed-ball chest pass, overhead slam3 × 5
Full bodyKettlebell swing; or 20–30 m (20–30 yd) sprints3 × 10; sprints 4–6 runs
  • Every rep fast and clean; stop when you slow down. This isn't cardio, so don't get out of breath.
  • Learn to land first: knees over toes, land softly. Heavier, or knees bothering you? Use med-ball throws instead of jumps.
  • Sprints: start at 70–80% speed and build up over a few weeks. If you've strained a hamstring before, sprint on a stationary bike instead.

Mobility: move before, stretch after

  • Before: dynamic warm-up (30–60 s each): hip circles, world's greatest stretch, thoracic rotations, band shoulder circles. Skip long static stretches before lifting; they briefly sap strength.
  • After: static stretches (30–60 s each): stretch what you trained, never into pain. If you sit a lot, pick two a day: hip flexors, chest, upper back, ankles.

Balance and core: built into lifting

  • At least 2 single-leg exercises a week: Bulgarian split squat, single-leg Romanian deadlift, step-up.
  • One core exercise per session, 2–3 sets: Pallof press, one-arm farmer's carry, plank, dead bug. These teach your trunk to stay still, which protects your back better than crunches.
  • Sneak it into daily life: stand on one leg for 30 s while brushing your teeth; close your eyes once that's easy.

Chapter 7

Putting the week together

About 5–6 hours a week covers all six elements. Here's a sample week using the standard 3-day plan. Shuffle the days to fit your schedule.

Scheduling tips

  • Leave about 48 hours before training the same muscle again. Shorter is fine if your next session doesn't suffer.
  • Try not to run or do intervals the day after legs; cycling and brisk walking matter less.
  • Finish hard training 1–2 hours before bed if you can.
  • If your week gets disrupted, just shift things along. No need to make up missed sessions.
The busy-week minimum

2–3 full-body lifting sessions (40 min each, main lifts only) + 1–2 cardio sessions of 30 min. Holding steady is a win.

Chapter 8

Eating

Eat enough protein, set calories to match your goal, and keep it about 80% whole foods and 20% flexible.

Protein

  • 1.6–2.2 g per kg of body weight a day (0.7–1 g per lb). 1.6 is enough for most people. Spread it over 3–4 meals.
  • If you're overweight, use your goal weight, not your current weight. Example: goal 70 kg (154 lb) → about 110–150 g a day, 30–40 g per meal.
  • Sources: eggs, chicken breast, lean beef, fish, tofu, plain yogurt, milk. Fill gaps with whey protein.

Calories: start from where you are

Normal weight, not much muscle, new to liftingMaintenanceFocus on training and your shape will improve; no need to diet

  • The closer you get, the slower you go: if you're already lean and want to keep your muscle, about 0.5% a week is plenty. At 70 kg, that's about 0.35 kg (0.75 lb).
  • On long cuts, return to maintenance for 1–2 weeks every 8–12 weeks to give body and mind a break.
  • Fat at least 20% of calories; fiber 25–35 g a day; 3 servings of vegetables and 2 of fruit.

Flexible rules

  • 80% whole foods, 20% foods you love. No need to quit sugar; keep added sugar under 10% of daily calories.
  • Plan dinners out and holidays into the week. The next day, eat normally. No skipping meals to compensate.
  • Go easy on alcohol: it's calorie-dense and hurts sleep and recovery.

  • Fat burners and “testosterone boosters”: weak evidence; some contain unlisted ingredients that can harm you.

  • Caffeine: no more than about 400 mg a day (3–4 cups of coffee); about 200 mg if pregnant.
  • Choose third-party tested products, such as NSF Certified for Sport or Informed Sport.
Chapter 9

Sleep and recovery

Training is the stimulus; progress happens while you rest. If recovery can't keep up, more training only takes you backward.

Sleep: 7–9 hours a night

Short sleep drags down strength, muscle gain, and appetite control together, and you lose more muscle while cutting.

Routine
  • Wake within the same 1-hour window every day, weekends included
  • Put the phone down 30–60 min before bed, or at least dim it
  • Finish hard training 1–2 hours before bed
Environment and drinks
  • Dark, quiet, cool room (about 18–22°C / 64–72°F)
  • No caffeine in the 8 hours before bed
  • Alcohol disrupts deep sleep; keep it low while training

Managing recovery

  • Several nights under 6 hours: train lighter that week. Don't push through.
  • High stress at work or home: cut training volume by 20–30% and just hold steady. Stress stacks; your body can't tell which kind it is.
  • While cutting: deficits beyond 25% usually hit recovery and strength hard.
  • Deload weeks: about every 6–8 weeks, or earlier when fatigue builds (see Chapter 3).
Fatigue check (not a diagnosis)

If two or more of these show up, rest 2–3 days, then train easy for a week:

  • Morning resting heart rate clearly higher than usual
  • Strength sliding for two weeks straight
  • Poor sleep, low motivation, getting sick easily

If rest doesn't help, or you also notice the “signals that matter more than body fat” from Chapter 1, see a doctor.

When to change the plan

Wait until numbers stall for 2–3 weeks; don't change course over a day or two of noise. Change one thing at a time (for example, 150–200 fewer calories a day) and watch for another 2–3 weeks.

Chapter 11

Maintenance and mindset

The years after you reach your goal are the real point of this approach. Maintaining is far easier than chasing, as long as your lifestyle can carry it.

Training for maintenance

Eating for maintenance

  • Once you reach your goal, return to maintenance calories for your new weight. All at once or a little each week both work; fine-tune using 2–4 weeks of weight trend.
  • Body fat drifting 2–3% up and down is normal. After travel, dinners out, or busy stretches, just return to your rhythm. No compensating.

Keep the good parts

The feeling that effort pays off, and getting used to a little discomfort, are real gifts of training. Keep them. But there's no need to grade your body with scores or tiers; that turns into anxiety fast.

Warning signs for your mindset

If any of these sound familiar, chasing a physique has started to hurt your life. Ease your standards, and talk to a professional if you need to:

  • Harsh guilt after unplanned food, or exercising hard to “burn it off.”
  • Turning down meals with friends to protect your diet.
  • Checking the mirror and scale over and over, with the number setting your mood for the day.
